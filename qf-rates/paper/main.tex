\begin{abstract}
Numerical implementations of the same interest-rate model can disagree because
of integration rules, discretization, conventions, calibration choices, or
exercise-policy estimation. This paper asks how much confidence can be assigned
to G2++ swaption prices and risks when these error sources are made explicit. A
reproducible validation protocol compares three-dimensional Gauss--Hermite
quadrature, independently time-stepped Monte Carlo, and QuantLib. The external
comparison spans \QuantLibGridCells{} European payer swaptions across five
parameter regimes, three expiries, three tenors, and three moneyness levels.
Among the \QuantLibGridMaterialCells{} cells worth at least one basis point of
notional in QuantLib, the absolute relative price difference has median
\QuantLibGridMedianRelativeError{} and 95th percentile
\QuantLibGridNinetyFifthRelativeError; the maximum absolute price gap over the full grid
is \QuantLibGridMaxDifferenceBp{} basis points of notional. Fixed-strike curve
DV01 and a controlled joint G2++ volatility bump agree within
\RiskMaxDVOneDifference{} and \RiskMaxVolatilityDifference, respectively, over
four representative cases. Coupled paths isolate time-discretization bias,
while path-count experiments report Monte Carlo confidence intervals. Eight
calibration starts expose local-solution and parameter-identification risk, and
Longstaff--Schwartz policies are evaluated on paths independent of their
training samples. The accompanying \texttt{qf-rates} C++20 artifact regenerates
all tests, CSV evidence, tables, and figures. The contribution is not a new
short-rate model; it is a transparent error-budget and cross-engine validation
study whose scope and failure modes can be independently audited.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

The mathematical description of an interest-rate model is only one part of a
usable pricing system. A practical implementation also fixes curve conventions,
cash-flow signs, interpolation, schedule generation, random-number handling,
integration rules, optimizer bounds, and reporting units. Two programs that claim
to implement the same model can consequently disagree for reasons that have
nothing to do with the stochastic differential equations. The discrepancy is
harder to diagnose when the implementation is hidden inside a monolithic
application or a notebook with implicit state.

This paper studies those numerical disagreements for G2++ European and Bermudan
swaptions. The associated \texttt{qf-rates} C++20 library is the executable
research artifact, not the research question. It makes curve conventions,
quadrature, simulation, calibration, bump-and-revalue risk, and exercise-policy
estimation inspectable. The intended use is research, education, and numerical
prototyping; it is not presented as a production trade-capture, collateral,
regulatory-capital, or market-data system.

The scientific value of the artifact is primarily methodological. First, the
material G2++ European price is reached by two numerical routes that do not share
their central integration logic: deterministic joint-Gaussian quadrature and
time-stepped Monte Carlo. Second, European swaption prices are compared with
QuantLib \citep{quantlib} over a predeclared 135-cell
expiry--tenor--moneyness--parameter grid, and selected price discrepancies are
propagated into finite-difference sensitivities. Third, stochastic claims are supported
by measured standard errors and fixed convergence grids rather than a single
seed. Fourth, paper tables and figures are generated from committed CSV files
that the validation executable can reproduce. These practices follow the
general principle that computational claims should be tied to executable,
versioned evidence \citep{sandve2013}.

The specific contributions are:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item a convention-aligned, \QuantLibGridCells-cell external validation grid
  spanning expiry, tenor, moneyness, and five G2++ parameter regimes;
  \item an error-propagation experiment comparing fixed-strike curve DV01 and a
  joint model-volatility bump across two independent implementations;
  \item an order-8 tensor Gauss--Hermite engine checked by independent Monte
  Carlo, including confidence-interval and coupled time-step convergence tests;
  \item deterministic multi-start calibration and Longstaff--Schwartz policy
  evaluation on simulation paths independent of the training sample;
  \item measured variance-reduction and wrong-way-risk sensitivity experiments;
  \item an end-to-end C++20 reproducibility artifact comprising tests, CSV
  output, plotting code, metadata, continuous integration, and an arXiv source
  package.
\end{enumerate}

No claim of a new pricing model or closed-form formula is made. The objective is
to show what can be concluded from a compact open implementation, what remains
implementation-specific, and how numerical evidence should be bounded.

\subsection{Research questions and evidence design}

The study is organized around four questions. First, after conventions are
aligned, how closely do independent G2++ European swaption engines agree across
expiry, tenor, strike, and parameter regime? Second, do remaining pricing gaps
materially affect curve and volatility sensitivities? Third, how much additional
uncertainty is introduced by Monte Carlo time discretization, calibration
initialization, regression bases, random seeds, and path reuse? Fourth, can every
reported result be regenerated by a versioned pipeline?

These questions determine the hierarchy of evidence. Exact equalities are used
when the mathematical object admits them: curve-node reproduction, swap par
value, put--call parity, and the G2++ initial term structure. Statistical
intervals are used for simulation output. Cross-implementation comparisons are
performed only after curve, schedule, compounding, and notional conventions have
been aligned. Optimizer output is treated as a set of candidate local solutions,
not as proof of parameter identification. LSM policies are evaluated both on
their training paths and on independently simulated paths. Finally, the complete
stress surface is retained rather than presenting only a favorable slice.

\section{Related work}

Bachelier's early treatment of speculation introduced a normal model of price
increments \citep{bachelier1900}. In modern fixed-income practice, the Bachelier
formula remains useful for options quoted in normal volatility, including
negative-rate regimes. Black's forward option model \citep{black1976} supplies
the corresponding lognormal convention used here for Black--76 prices and
Greeks.

The G2++ model is a two-factor Gaussian short-rate model in which a deterministic
shift fits the initial term structure while correlated Ornstein--Uhlenbeck
factors drive future rates. Its standard construction and bond-pricing
identities are described in \citet{brigo2006}. Gaussian structure makes bond
prices exponentially affine in the factors and permits accurate low-dimensional
quadrature for European claims. American and Bermudan claims do not generally
retain the same simple integration structure. The least-squares Monte Carlo
method of \citet{longstaff2001} instead estimates continuation values by
regression on simulated state variables.

Monte Carlo error estimation, antithetic variables, control variates, and
discretization analysis are treated comprehensively by \citet{glasserman2003}.
The present implementation uses online moments based on Welford's recurrence
\citep{welford1962}, reporting the sample standard error with each stochastic
price. Numerical stability matters beyond variance estimation: linear systems,
optimization, interpolation, and finite differences can all create apparently
plausible prices while losing meaningful digits \citep{higham2002}. This
motivates the combination of exact identities, independent implementations,
convergence experiments, and external cross-checks used below.

Calibration introduces a distinct inverse problem. Even when every forward
price is evaluated accurately, short-rate parameters may be weakly identified:
different combinations of mean reversion, factor volatility, and correlation
can generate similar swaption prices. A small residual therefore does not by
itself imply a unique or economically stable parameter vector. The present
paper makes this distinction operational by retaining all seeded starts and
their terminal solutions. The diagnostic is intentionally more modest than a
global-optimality claim.

For early-exercise claims, regression creates another separation between a
pricing algorithm and its statistical assessment. The backward LSM recursion
constructs an exercise policy from simulated observations. Reusing those same
observations for valuation entangles policy selection and performance
measurement. Independent-path evaluation instead estimates the value of a
frozen policy conditional on the training sample. It does not remove
approximation error or guarantee an optimal stopping rule, but it makes one
source of finite-sample uncertainty visible.

The study also follows a software-artifact perspective. Rather than treating
source code as supplementary prose, each numerical claim is connected to a CSV
row emitted by the validation executable, a plotting function, and an
acceptance criterion. This design permits reviewers to inspect the mapping from
model assumptions to code and from code to manuscript values.

\section{Conventions and deterministic instruments}
\label{sec:conventions}

\subsection{Notation, units, and claim boundary}

All random variables are defined on a filtered probability space under the
risk-neutral measure $\mathbb{Q}$. Time $t$ is a non-negative year fraction
measured from valuation. $P(t,T)$ denotes the time-$t$ value of a zero-coupon
bond paying one unit at $T\geq t$. Rates and volatilities are stored as decimal
annualized quantities; one basis point is $10^{-4}$. Prices in the paper use
unit notional unless a currency notional is stated explicitly.

The symbols $a$ and $b$ denote positive mean-reversion speeds, while $\sigma$
and $\eta$ denote factor volatilities. The instantaneous Brownian correlation
is $\rho$. A factor state is written $X_t=(x_t,y_t)^\top$. $\Phi$ and $\phi$
denote the standard normal distribution and density. For a finite sample,
$\widehat{\operatorname{Var}}$ is the unbiased sample variance and $SE$ is the
standard error of the reported estimator, not the standard deviation of a
single path payoff.

This notation deliberately excludes calendar objects. A time such as $2.0$
means exactly two model years, not a date obtained after a holiday adjustment.
The boundary matters: the paper validates numerical finance logic under explicit
year-fraction conventions, not production lifecycle behavior.

\subsection{Curves}

Time is measured in year fractions from valuation. A continuously compounded
zero rate $z(T)$ defines
\begin{equation}
  P(0,T)=\exp[-z(T)T].
\end{equation}
The library supports a flat analytic curve and an interpolated discount curve.
Supplied maturities must be strictly increasing and discount factors strictly
positive. Linear discount interpolation and log-linear discount interpolation
are available; the latter produces piecewise-constant continuously compounded
forward rates. Beyond the final node, the terminal zero rate is held flat.

The curve interface is deliberately narrow: discount factors, zero rates, and
forwards. This permits a discount curve and a forward curve to be supplied
separately at the swap boundary even though the paper experiments use a single
curve. For coupon period $[T_i,T_{i+1}]$ with accrual $\alpha_i$, the simple
forward is
\begin{equation}
  L_i = \frac{P_f(0,T_i)/P_f(0,T_{i+1})-1}{\alpha_i}.
\end{equation}
All paper schedules are exact year-fraction schedules. There are no calendar
dates, holiday rules, stubs, day-count bases, or business-day adjustments.
This restriction is central to interpreting the QuantLib comparison.

\subsection{Swaps and analytic options}

For notional $N$, fixed coupon $K$, discount curve $P_d$, and payment dates
$T_i$, the unsigned fixed-leg present value is
\begin{equation}
  PV_{\mathrm{fixed}}=N K \sum_i \alpha_i P_d(0,T_i)=NKA.
\end{equation}
The floating leg discounts the forward-curve-implied coupons. Under a single
curve and zero spread, it telescopes to
$N[P(0,T_0)-P(0,T_n)]$. Tests verify both this identity and that a swap struck
at par has zero NPV. A payer swap pays fixed and receives floating; NPV signs
are from the holder's perspective.

\begin{proposition}[Single-curve floating-leg telescoping]
\label{prop:telescoping}
For a zero-spread floating leg with simple forwards extracted from the same
curve used for discounting,
\[
  PV_{\mathrm{float}}
  =N\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}\alpha_i L_iP(0,T_{i+1})
  =N\{P(0,T_0)-P(0,T_n)\}.
\]
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Substitution of
$\alpha_iL_i=P(0,T_i)/P(0,T_{i+1})-1$ turns each discounted coupon into
$P(0,T_i)-P(0,T_{i+1})$. Summation cancels every interior discount factor.
\end{proof}

The proposition is both a financial identity and an implementation diagnostic.
A failure can reveal an accrual mismatch, an incorrect payment discount,
inconsistent forward and discount curves, or an off-by-one schedule error. The
test is therefore more informative than comparing a single swap NPV with a
stored number.

The Black--76 payer-call value is
\begin{equation}
  DF\,N\left[F\Phi(d_1)-K\Phi(d_2)\right],\qquad
  d_{1,2}=\frac{\log(F/K)\pm\frac12\sigma^2T}{\sigma\sqrt T}.
\end{equation}
The Bachelier call value is
\begin{equation}
  DF\,N\left[(F-K)\Phi(d)+\sigma_N\sqrt T\,\phi(d)\right],\qquad
  d=\frac{F-K}{\sigma_N\sqrt T}.
\end{equation}
Puts use a sign transformation; price, delta, gamma, and vega are returned
together. Tests cover known values, put--call parity, monotonicity,
non-negativity, negative forwards under Bachelier, and degenerate expiry or
volatility.

\section{G2++ model and European pricing}
\label{sec:g2}

\subsection{Dynamics and bond prices}

Under the risk-neutral measure, the short rate is
\begin{align}
  r_t &= \varphi(t)+x_t+y_t,\\
  dx_t &= -a x_t\,dt+\sigma\,dW_t^x,\\
  dy_t &= -b y_t\,dt+\eta\,dW_t^y,\qquad
  dW_t^x dW_t^y=\rho\,dt,
\end{align}
with $a,b>0$, $\sigma,\eta\geq0$, and $-1<\rho<1$. Define
\begin{equation}
  B(k,\tau)=\frac{1-e^{-k\tau}}{k}.
\end{equation}
The time-$t$ zero-coupon bond is
\begin{equation}
  P(t,T)=A(t,T)
  \exp[-B(a,T-t)x_t-B(b,T-t)y_t],
\end{equation}
where
\begin{equation}
  A(t,T)=\frac{P(0,T)}{P(0,t)}
  \exp\left[\frac12\{V(T-t)-V(T)+V(t)\}\right].
\end{equation}
\subsubsection{Integrated variance and deterministic shift}

Let
\[
  I_t=\int_0^t(x_s+y_s)\,ds,\qquad V(t)=\operatorname{Var}(I_t).
\]
The implementation evaluates the following closed-form expression:
\begin{align}
V(t)={}&\frac{\sigma^2}{a^2}
\left[t-\frac{2(1-e^{-at})}{a}+\frac{1-e^{-2at}}{2a}\right]\notag\\
&+\frac{\eta^2}{b^2}
\left[t-\frac{2(1-e^{-bt})}{b}+\frac{1-e^{-2bt}}{2b}\right]\notag\\
&+\frac{2\rho\sigma\eta}{ab}
\left[t-\frac{1-e^{-at}}{a}-\frac{1-e^{-bt}}{b}
+\frac{1-e^{-(a+b)t}}{a+b}\right].
\label{eq:integrated-variance}
\end{align}
The line breaks separate the $x$, $y$, and cross-factor contributions. The
deterministic shift is equivalently
\begin{equation}
  \varphi(t)=f^{M}(0,t)+\frac12 V'(t),\qquad
  f^{M}(0,t)=-\partial_t\log P^{M}(0,t),
\end{equation}
where the superscript $M$ denotes the supplied market curve. In code, the curve
derivative is evaluated through a short finite difference because the public
curve interface exposes discount factors rather than an analytic forward
derivative.

\begin{proposition}[Exact initial term-structure fit]
\label{prop:initial-fit}
If $x_0=y_0=0$ and $A(t,T)$ is defined above, then the model bond at valuation
satisfies $P(0,T)=P^{M}(0,T)$ for every admissible maturity $T$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Set $t=0$ in the affine bond formula. Since $V(0)=0$ and
$V(T-0)-V(T)=0$, the exponential adjustment in $A(0,T)$ equals one.
The factor exponential also equals one because $x_0=y_0=0$. Thus
$P(0,T)=P^{M}(0,T)/P^{M}(0,0)=P^{M}(0,T)$, using $P^{M}(0,0)=1$.
\end{proof}

This is an exact construction property rather than a calibration result. The
corresponding unit test evaluates several maturities and requires agreement near
machine precision. It detects errors in the variance adjustment or affine
loading that a swaption-only comparison could conceal.

\subsubsection{Exact factor transition}

For a step $\Delta>0$, conditional means are
\[
  \mathbb{E}_t[x_{t+\Delta}]=e^{-a\Delta}x_t,\qquad
  \mathbb{E}_t[y_{t+\Delta}]=e^{-b\Delta}y_t,
\]
and the innovation covariance matrix has entries
\begin{align}
  q_x(\Delta)&=\frac{\sigma^2}{2a}(1-e^{-2a\Delta}),\\
  q_y(\Delta)&=\frac{\eta^2}{2b}(1-e^{-2b\Delta}),\\
  q_{xy}(\Delta)&=\frac{\rho\sigma\eta}{a+b}
     (1-e^{-(a+b)\Delta}).
\end{align}
Hence the finite-step innovation correlation is
\begin{equation}
  \rho_\Delta=\frac{q_{xy}(\Delta)}
  {\sqrt{q_x(\Delta)q_y(\Delta)}}.
\end{equation}
Except when $a=b$ or in the infinitesimal-step limit,
$\rho_\Delta$ need not equal $\rho$. The simulator draws independent standard
normals $Z_1,Z_2$ and uses
\[
  \varepsilon_x=\sqrt{q_x}Z_1,\qquad
  \varepsilon_y=\sqrt{q_y}
  \{\rho_\Delta Z_1+\sqrt{1-\rho_\Delta^2}Z_2\}.
\]
No Euler approximation is used for factor endpoints. Time discretization enters
only through the accumulated short-rate integral.

\begin{proposition}[Admissibility of the transition covariance]
For $a,b>0$, $\sigma,\eta\geq0$, and $|\rho|<1$, the finite-step innovation
covariance is positive semidefinite for every $\Delta\geq0$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
The innovation vector is an It\^o integral of deterministic kernels against a
two-dimensional Brownian motion with positive-semidefinite instantaneous
correlation matrix. Its covariance is therefore an integral of
positive-semidefinite matrices. Equivalently, Cauchy--Schwarz gives
$q_{xy}^2\le q_xq_y$.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Joint-Gaussian quadrature}

For expiry $T_e$ and final swap payment $T_n$, the payer swap value per unit
notional at expiry is
\begin{equation}
  S(T_e)=1-P(T_e,T_n)
  -K\sum_i \alpha_i P(T_e,T_i).
\end{equation}
Pricing requires both the terminal factors and the discount accumulated to
expiry. Under G2++, the vector
\begin{equation}
  Z=
  \left(
    \int_0^{T_e}(x_s+y_s)\,ds,\,
    x_{T_e},\,
    y_{T_e}
  \right)
\end{equation}
is jointly Gaussian with zero mean and covariance
\begin{equation}
  \Sigma(T_e)=
  \begin{pmatrix}
    V(T_e) & c_x(T_e) & c_y(T_e)\\
    c_x(T_e) & v_x(T_e) & v_{xy}(T_e)\\
    c_y(T_e) & v_{xy}(T_e) & v_y(T_e)
  \end{pmatrix},
  \label{eq:joint-covariance}
\end{equation}
where
\begin{align}
v_x(t)&=\frac{\sigma^2}{2a}(1-e^{-2at}),&
v_y(t)&=\frac{\eta^2}{2b}(1-e^{-2bt}),\\
v_{xy}(t)&=\frac{\rho\sigma\eta}{a+b}(1-e^{-(a+b)t}),\\
c_x(t)&=\int_0^t\operatorname{Cov}(x_s+y_s,x_t)\,ds,&
c_y(t)&=\int_0^t\operatorname{Cov}(x_s+y_s,y_t)\,ds.
\end{align}
The factor entries are closed form. The two one-dimensional cross-covariance
integrals are evaluated independently with adaptive Simpson integration at
tolerance $10^{-11}$. Appendix~\ref{app:moments} gives their closed forms, which
provide an analytical audit of the numerical integrands.

Let $L$ be the lower Cholesky factor of $\Sigma$, let
$u_i,w_i$, $i=1,\ldots,8$, be the physicists' Gauss--Hermite nodes and weights,
and let $z_{ijk}=\sqrt{2}(u_i,u_j,u_k)^\top$. If
$Lz_{ijk}=(I_{ijk},x_{ijk},y_{ijk})^\top$, the implemented price is
\begin{equation}
\widehat{V}_{GH}
=NP^{M}(0,T_e)e^{-V(T_e)/2}\pi^{-3/2}
\sum_{i,j,k=1}^{8}w_iw_jw_k e^{-I_{ijk}}
\left[S(T_e;x_{ijk},y_{ijk})\right]^+ .
\label{eq:gh-price}
\end{equation}
The factor $e^{-V(T_e)/2}$ belongs to the deterministic shift, while
$e^{-I_{ijk}}$ discounts the stochastic factor integral. Omitting either term
changes the measure-weighted payoff.

\begin{proposition}[Gaussian state reduction]
For a European swaption with deterministic payment schedule under G2++, the
time-zero price depends on the path before expiry only through the
three-dimensional Gaussian vector in~\eqref{eq:joint-covariance}.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
The accumulated discount factor is
$\exp[-\int_0^{T_e}\varphi(s)\,ds-I_{T_e}]$. Every expiry bond entering the
swap value is exponentially affine in $(x_{T_e},y_{T_e})$. The discounted
payoff is therefore a measurable function of
$(I_{T_e},x_{T_e},y_{T_e})$ alone. Joint Gaussianity follows because each
component is a linear functional of the Brownian drivers.
\end{proof}

The resulting deterministic algorithm has five steps:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item validate the schedule, expiry, notional, and quadrature order;
  \item assemble $\Sigma(T_e)$ and compute its Cholesky factor;
  \item map each of the 512 tensor nodes into $(I,x,y)$;
  \item build every expiry bond and positive swap payoff;
  \item multiply by stochastic and deterministic discount factors and sum the
  weighted nodes.
\end{enumerate}
This construction avoids an inner time discretization in the production
European price. The current API accepts order eight only; unsupported orders
are rejected rather than silently mapped to a different computation. Fixing the
order narrows the validation claim but also prevents an untested configuration
from appearing equivalent to the published experiment.

\subsection{Independent Monte Carlo route}

The validation engine evolves the two OU factors with exact finite-step
marginals, integrates the short rate by the trapezoid rule, and values the
terminal swap from model bonds. Thus the factor endpoint simulation is exact,
while the path discount integral has time-discretization error. Antithetic
innovations are enabled in the convergence experiment. For discounted payoffs
$X_1,\ldots,X_n$, the program reports
\begin{equation}
  \widehat{P}_n=\frac1n\sum_{j=1}^n X_j,\qquad
  SE(\widehat{P}_n)=\sqrt{\frac{\widehat{\mathrm{Var}}(X)}{n}},
\end{equation}
with a nominal 95\% interval $\widehat{P}_n\pm1.96SE$. Moments are updated
online, avoiding a cancellation-prone difference of large sums.

Figure~\ref{fig:mc} reports two independent fixed seeds. At 30,000 paths, the
seed-42 estimate is \LargestMCPrice{} with standard error \LargestMCSE{},
compared with deterministic price \DeterministicPrice{}. The result is
consistent with convergence to the deterministic engine, while the seed-7
estimate illustrates that a single simulation run should not be treated as a
pointwise proof.

\subsubsection{Standardized-error diagnostic}

Visual overlap of confidence intervals is useful but incomplete. For each run
$r$, define
\begin{equation}
  Z_r=\frac{\widehat{P}_r-P_{GH}}{SE_r},
\end{equation}
where $P_{GH}$ is the deterministic quadrature price. Under an idealized
unbiased normal approximation, values between $-1.96$ and $1.96$ are individually
compatible with a two-sided 95\% interval. These eight observations are
dependent across path counts because the same seed restarts each sequence; they
must not be interpreted as eight independent coverage trials.

Figure~\ref{fig:mc-z} shows that \MCCoveredRuns{} of \MCTotalRuns{} reported
values lie inside the nominal band and the maximum absolute standardized error
is \MCMaxAbsZ. The diagnostic separates price scale from sampling scale and
makes the mild negative tendency for seed 7 visible. It does not establish

With antithetic sampling, one statistical observation is the average of a path
and its sign-reversed counterpart. Thus a nominal configuration with $n$ paths
contains $n/2$ pair averages when $n$ is even. The standard error is computed
from those pair averages, preserving their within-pair dependence. Treating the
two legs as independent observations would understate uncertainty.

\subsection{Coupled time-discretization convergence}

A naive time-step comparison changes both the discretization and the random
sample, so ordinary Monte Carlo noise can dominate the difference between
grids. The coupled experiment instead simulates each factor path once on a
384-step grid and reuses the same factor states and terminal payoff at
12, 24, 48, 96, 192, and 384 integration steps. Only the trapezoidal
short-rate integral changes. For level $h$ and finest level $f$, the program
reports the paired estimator
\begin{equation}
  \widehat{B}_{h,f}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}
  \left(X_j^{(h)}-X_j^{(f)}\right),
\end{equation}
and its standard error from the pathwise differences. Common random numbers
therefore remove most payoff sampling variation from the discretization
comparison.

\begin{proposition}[Variance of the coupled difference]
For square-integrable coarse and fine estimators evaluated on common paths,
\[
  \operatorname{Var}(X^{(h)}-X^{(f)})
  =\operatorname{Var}(X^{(h)})+\operatorname{Var}(X^{(f)})
   -2\operatorname{Cov}(X^{(h)},X^{(f)}).
\]
When the two discretizations are strongly positively correlated, the paired
standard error can be much smaller than the standard error of either price.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
The identity follows by expanding the centered square. Positive covariance
subtracts directly from the variance of the difference.
\end{proof}

The finest path supplies the terminal factor state and payoff used at every
level. Coarser grids retain only fine-grid endpoints whose indices are multiples
of the required stride. Consequently, the paired difference isolates the
trapezoidal integral and does not mix it with a different payoff state.

Figure~\ref{fig:time-convergence} shows both fixed seeds. Across every coarser
grid, the largest absolute paired bias is \TimeMaxPairedBias{} price units,
equivalent to \TimeMaxPairedBiasBp{} basis points of notional. The sampling
difference from deterministic quadrature remains larger, confirming why an
uncoupled comparison would not resolve the integration bias reliably. This
result applies to the declared instrument and parameter set, not to all G2++
workloads.

\section{Calibration and Bermudan exercise}
\label{sec:calibration}

\subsection{Normal-volatility calibration}

A market quote contains expiry, underlying tenor, strike, absolute normal
volatility, and weight. For each parameter candidate, the calibration procedure
builds an annual schedule, computes the G2++ quadrature price, inverts the
Bachelier formula by bracketed bisection, and minimizes weighted normal-volatility
RMSE. Parameter bounds are
\begin{equation}
\begin{split}
  a&\in[0.005,1],\qquad b\in[0.01,1.5],\\
  \sigma,\eta&\in[10^{-4},0.1],\qquad
  \rho\in[-0.95,0.95].
\end{split}
\end{equation}

For quote $q$, let $C_q^{G2}(\theta)$ be the quadrature price at parameter
vector $\theta=(a,b,\sigma,\eta,\rho)$, and let
$C_q^{N}(\nu)$ be the corresponding Bachelier price as a function of absolute
normal volatility. The model-implied volatility is the root
\begin{equation}
  \nu_q(\theta)=\{\nu\geq0:
  C_q^{N}(\nu)=C_q^{G2}(\theta)\}.
\end{equation}
The Bachelier price is monotone in $\nu$ for non-degenerate claims, so a
bracketed bisection gives a transparent inversion. Starting from an upper
volatility of 1\%, the code doubles the bracket until the model price is
enclosed, then solves to tolerance $10^{-9}$.

The reported objective is
\begin{equation}
  \operatorname{RMSE}(\theta)=
  \left\{\frac{\sum_{q=1}^{Q}w_q
  [\nu_q(\theta)-\nu_q^{M}]^2}{\sum_{q=1}^{Q}w_q}\right\}^{1/2}.
  \label{eq:calibration-objective}
\end{equation}
All six synthetic quotes use unit weight. Consequently, the objective has the
same absolute units as normal volatility; multiplication by $10^4$ converts it
to normal-volatility basis points.

A deterministic bounded coordinate search is used. It is slower and less
sophisticated than gradient-based least squares, but its steps and constraints
are easy to audit.

At each iteration, the solver evaluates positive and negative moves along each
coordinate, projects trial values to their bounds, accepts the best improving
candidate, and halves step lengths when no coordinate improves the objective.
It stops after 120 iterations or once the maximum step is below
$2\times10^{-5}$. The boolean convergence flag therefore records the numerical
stopping rule; it is not a statement about global optimality.

The paper calibration uses six quotes: expiries 1Y, 2Y, and 3Y crossed with 5Y
and 10Y tenors. \texttt{qf-rates} attains an absolute normal-volatility RMSE of
$1.8688\times10^{-4}$; the aligned QuantLib exercise attains
$3.8671\times10^{-4}$. These values should not be interpreted as an optimizer
ranking. The parameter vectors differ materially, indicating weak identification
on the sparse grid and sensitivity to parameterization, bounds, and solver. The
validation claim is limited to constrained finite parameters and acceptable
reproduction of the input volatility surface.

\subsection{Deterministic multi-start calibration}

To expose optimizer dependence directly, the calibration API now runs the
default parameter vector plus seven seeded, log-uniform starts within the
declared parameter bounds. Each run retains its initial point, final parameters,
RMSE, iterations, and convergence flag; the minimum-RMSE run is selected without
discarding the alternatives.

For the four positive parameters, a seeded uniform draw $U\sim U(0,1)$ is
mapped to
\begin{equation}
  \theta_0=\exp\{\log \ell+U(\log u-\log \ell)\},
\end{equation}
where $\ell,u$ are the declared lower and upper bounds. Correlation is sampled
uniformly in level between $-0.95$ and $0.95$. Log-uniform starts allocate
comparable probability mass across orders of magnitude and are more revealing
for volatility parameters than level-uniform draws.

Figure~\ref{fig:multistart} shows substantial dependence on the initial point.
The default start produces RMSE \MultiStartDefaultRMSE{}, while run
\MultiStartBestRun{} produces \MultiStartBestRMSE{}, an improvement of
\MultiStartImprovement{}. This is evidence that a single local coordinate
search understates optimization uncertainty. It does not resolve structural
parameter identification: multiple parameter vectors can still generate similar
prices on six quotes.

\subsection{Longstaff--Schwartz}

For multiple exercise dates, simulated factor states and cumulative discount
factors are stored at the exercise grid. Backward induction regresses discounted
realized continuation on either a linear basis or the quadratic state basis
\begin{equation}
  (1,x,y,x^2,xy,y^2).
\end{equation}

Let exercise dates be $t_1<\cdots<t_M$, $H_m^{(j)}$ the intrinsic value on
path $j$, and $D_m^{(j)}$ the time-zero discount factor. If a later path value
$V_{m+1}^{(j)}$ is stored at $t_{m+1}$, its continuation target at $t_m$ is
\begin{equation}
  Y_m^{(j)}
  =V_{m+1}^{(j)}\frac{D_{m+1}^{(j)}}{D_m^{(j)}}.
\end{equation}
On in-the-money paths $\mathcal I_m=\{j:H_m^{(j)}>0\}$, coefficients solve the
ridge-stabilized normal equations
\begin{equation}
  (\Psi_m^\top\Psi_m+10^{-12}I)\widehat{\beta}_m
  =\Psi_m^\top Y_m.
  \label{eq:lsm-normal}
\end{equation}
Here the rows of $\Psi_m$ are the selected basis functions evaluated at
$(x_{t_m}^{(j)},y_{t_m}^{(j)})$. At least twice as many in-the-money paths as
basis functions are required before a regression is accepted.

Only in-the-money paths enter the regression. Exercise occurs when intrinsic
value exceeds estimated continuation; values are rolled backward using
path-specific discount ratios.

The backward recursion is therefore:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item initialize each path with intrinsic value at $t_M$;
  \item for $m=M-1,\ldots,1$, discount the stored later value to $t_m$;
  \item fit~\eqref{eq:lsm-normal} on in-the-money paths;
  \item replace continuation by $H_m$ when
    $H_m>\psi(X_{t_m})^\top\widehat{\beta}_m$;
  \item discount the resulting path values to time zero and average.
\end{enumerate}
The reported standard error is the sample standard error of discounted realized
path values after applying the learned stopping decisions.

With a single exercise date, the implementation delegates to the validated
European quadrature engine. For multiple dates, a comparable first-date European
value is used as a lower-bound safeguard against finite-sample regression bias.
This preserves an important no-arbitrage ordering, but the safeguard can mask an
unstable continuation regression if considered in isolation. The study therefore
reports path count, seed, basis, price, and standard error in full.

Figure~\ref{fig:lsm} shows results at 2,000, 5,000, and 10,000 paths for seeds
7 and 42. Standard errors decrease from approximately $3.6\times10^{-4}$ at
2,000 paths to $1.6\times10^{-4}$ at 10,000 paths. At 10,000 paths, the four
estimates span 0.01366320 to 0.01384655. The gap between seeds is comparable to
the reported sampling uncertainty, whereas the difference between linear and
quadratic bases within each seed is smaller. The table in
Appendix~\ref{app:lsm} preserves all twelve observations.

\subsection{Out-of-sample exercise-policy evaluation}

In-sample LSM uses the same paths to fit continuation regressions and to value
the resulting exercise rule. To measure this reuse explicitly, the new engine
fits all backward-regression coefficients on a training simulation, freezes the
policy, and then applies it forward to a second simulation with a distinct
seed. No coefficients are re-estimated on valuation paths. Paths that never
exercise are reported separately instead of being assigned automatically to
the final date.

\begin{proposition}[Conditional validity of the policy estimator]
\label{prop:oos}
Condition on a fitted exercise policy $\widehat{\pi}$ obtained from the training
sample. If the valuation paths are independent and identically distributed
under the pricing measure, their discounted payoff average is an unbiased
estimator of the value $V(\widehat{\pi})$ of that fixed policy, with the usual
sample standard error.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Conditional on the training sample, $\widehat{\pi}$ is deterministic. Each
valuation path produces an identically distributed discounted payoff obtained
by applying that fixed stopping rule. Independence and linearity of expectation
give unbiasedness of the sample mean for $V(\widehat{\pi})$.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}
The proposition does not state that $V(\widehat{\pi})$ equals the optimal
Bermudan value. Approximation bias, basis misspecification, training-sample
variation, and numerical discounting remain. Independent evaluation isolates
performance measurement from policy fitting; it does not solve the optimal
stopping problem exactly.
\end{remark}

The experiment crosses 2,000, 5,000, and 10,000 training paths with two training
seeds and linear/quadratic bases, while using 10,000 independent valuation paths
per configuration. Independent prices range from \OOSPriceMinimum{} to
\OOSPriceMaximum{}. The largest absolute training-minus-valuation gap is
\OOSMaxGap{}. Figure~\ref{fig:lsm-oos} shows that the sign of the gap changes
with the training seed; therefore the finite-sample difference should not be
described mechanically as a positive look-ahead bias. The relevant conclusion
is that independent evaluation reveals policy uncertainty that an in-sample
price alone does not show.

\end{figure}

\section{Market risk, exposure, and wrong-way sensitivity}
\label{sec:risk}

\subsection{Bump-and-revalue market risk}

DV01 is defined by a one-basis-point downward shift in continuously compounded
zero rates:
\begin{equation}
  DV01 = PV(z-1\text{ bp})-PV(z).
\end{equation}
Both discount and forward curves are shifted in the single-curve helper.
Bucketed DV01 is required to reconcile with the parallel bump within 2\%.
Volatility scenarios separately require a payer swaption value to increase
under an up shock and decrease under a down shock. Bucket vega is reconciled
with the global bump to 0.1\%.

For bucket $k$ and bump size $\delta$, the central sensitivity convention is
\begin{equation}
  \Delta_k^{(\delta)}
  =\frac{PV(\theta+\delta e_k)-PV(\theta-\delta e_k)}{2\delta}.
\end{equation}
The public helpers return currency-valued bump effects rather than normalized
derivatives where that convention is more familiar to risk users. The tests
therefore compare like-for-like aggregation: the sum of local curve bump effects
with a parallel curve effect, and weighted volatility buckets with a global
volatility bump. Directional scenarios are tested separately from
first-order reconciliation because a scenario contains finite convexity.

\subsection{Exposure and XVA discretization}

The exposure extension revalues swaps along G2++ paths. With netting enabled,
positive and negative exposure are taken after portfolio aggregation. A
transparent sensitivity proxy multiplies positive exposure by
$\exp[\beta(x_t+y_t)]$.

Let $V_j(t_i)$ be the netted portfolio value on path $j$ at exposure time
$t_i$. The independent and proxy-weighted expected positive exposures are
\begin{align}
  EPE_0(t_i)&=\frac1n\sum_{j=1}^{n}[V_j(t_i)]^+,\\
  EPE_\beta(t_i)&=\frac1n\sum_{j=1}^{n}[V_j(t_i)]^+
  \exp\{\beta(x_j(t_i)+y_j(t_i))\}.
  \label{eq:wwr-epe}
\end{align}
With deterministic counterparty survival $S_c(t)$ and recovery $R$, the
discrete CVA is
\begin{equation}
  CVA_\beta=(1-R)\sum_i P(0,t_i)EPE_\beta(t_i)
  \{S_c(t_{i-1})-S_c(t_i)\}.
\end{equation}
The experiment uses $R=40\%$, a 2\% counterparty hazard rate, and six exposure
dates from 0.5Y to 5Y.

Figure~\ref{fig:wwr-grid} reports the full sensitivity grid
$\beta=0,5,\ldots,30$. CVA increases monotonically from
\IndependentCVA{} at $\beta=0$ to \WrongWayThirtyCVA{} at $\beta=30$, a
\WrongWayThirtyIncrease{} increase. At $\beta=20$, the value is
\WrongWayCVA{}, or \WrongWayIncrease{} above the independent case. The exposure
panels show that the multiplier changes the whole profile rather than a single
summary statistic.

  \label{fig:wwr-grid}

This is not a calibrated joint credit/rates model. The exponential multiplier
does not define a counterparty intensity, is not normalized to preserve an
unconditional hazard curve, and can become extreme outside the tested state
range. The grid establishes implementation direction, smooth sensitivity, and
reproducibility only. Appendix~\ref{app:wwr-table} contains every CVA value and
peak exposure.

\section{Limitations and model risk}
\label{sec:limitations}

The following limitations define the boundary of the evidence:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Market conventions.} Schedules are year fractions. Calendars,
  stubs, day counts, fixing lags, settlement, collateral discounting, and
  production multi-curve bootstrap are absent.
  \item \textbf{Quadrature.} The European engine fixes tensor order eight.
  It is checked by Monte Carlo and by a 135-cell external QuantLib surface, but
  the study does not vary quadrature order or QuantLib engine settings
  exhaustively. The observed differences therefore combine numerical-method and
  residual convention effects rather than identifying one unique cause.
  \item \textbf{Calibration.} Coordinate search is deterministic but can settle
  at local minima. Six normal-volatility quotes do not identify five G2++
  parameters robustly. Eight starts expose but do not eliminate local-minimum
  and identification risk; regularization and denser market grids remain needed.
  \item \textbf{LSM regression.} Normal equations with a small ridge are less
  stable than QR or SVD. The European lower-bound safeguard can hide poor
  continuation estimates in the legacy in-sample result. Independent policy
  evaluation removes path reuse but not regression approximation error.
  \item \textbf{Monte Carlo.} Confidence intervals quantify sampling error,
  not model error. The coupled time-step study isolates discount-integration
  error for one declared case; broader parameter and maturity coverage is needed.
  \item \textbf{Risk and XVA.} The XVA module uses deterministic hazards, no
  collateral agreement, a proxy WWR multiplier, illustrative SIMM weights, and
  a simplified funding calculation. The cross-engine volatility diagnostic
  bumps model parameters without recalibration and must not be interpreted as
  market-quote vega. The module must not be treated as regulatory or
  counterparty-risk infrastructure.
  \item \textbf{Performance.} The implementation favors clarity. It does not
  claim optimal vectorization, parallelism, cache layout, or accelerator use.
\end{itemize}

These constraints also indicate useful research extensions: arbitrary-order
Hermite rules; a decomposition of the remaining cross-engine error by numerical
setting; quote-recalibrated and adjoint Greeks; QR/SVD LSM with cross-fitting;
regularized calibration; multi-curve calibration; and exposure simulation under
a calibrated joint rates/credit model.

\section{Conclusion}

This paper presented a reproducible numerical-validation protocol for G2++
swaption pricing. Its central result is a convention-aligned external comparison
covering \QuantLibGridCells{} European swaptions rather than one favorable
instrument. Across material cells, the median absolute relative difference is
\QuantLibGridMedianRelativeError{} and the 95th percentile is
\QuantLibGridNinetyFifthRelativeError. The largest full-grid absolute gap is
\QuantLibGridMaxDifferenceBp{} basis points of notional. Direct validation of
finite-difference outputs further shows maximum discrepancies of
\RiskMaxDVOneDifference{} for fixed-strike curve DV01 and
\RiskMaxVolatilityDifference{} for the controlled model-volatility bump.

Independent Monte Carlo confidence intervals and coupled time grids distinguish
sampling noise from discount-integration error. Multi-start calibration exposes
local minima and weak parameter identification, while independent-path
Longstaff--Schwartz valuation avoids reporting a training-sample policy value as
an out-of-sample result. These experiments turn implementation choices into
observable parts of an error budget.

The central conclusion remains deliberately narrow: within the stated synthetic
conventions and test domain, the quadrature engine, QuantLib reference, Monte
Carlo implementation, calibration routine, and Bermudan exercise workflow form
a coherent but not perfectly identical numerical system. The remaining
differences are measured rather than hidden. The accompanying C++20 artifact
makes every reported result reviewable and reproducible; it does not eliminate
model risk, establish production readiness, or introduce new G2++ theory.

\section{Analytical Gaussian moments}
\label{app:moments}

This appendix derives the entries used in the joint state
matrix~\eqref{eq:joint-covariance}. With zero initial state,
\begin{align}
  x_t&=\sigma\int_0^t e^{-a(t-u)}\,dW_u^x,\\
  y_t&=\eta\int_0^t e^{-b(t-u)}\,dW_u^y.
\end{align}
Integration in time and stochastic Fubini give
\begin{align}
  \int_0^t x_s\,ds
  &=\frac{\sigma}{a}\int_0^t
    (1-e^{-a(t-u)})\,dW_u^x,\\
  \int_0^t y_s\,ds
  &=\frac{\eta}{b}\int_0^t
    (1-e^{-b(t-u)})\,dW_u^y.
\end{align}
It\^o isometry immediately yields the two single-factor contributions and the
cross term in~\eqref{eq:integrated-variance}.

The covariance between the integrated factor sum and the terminal $x$ factor is
\begin{align}
c_x(t)={}&
\frac{\sigma^2}{a}
\left[\frac{1-e^{-at}}{a}-\frac{1-e^{-2at}}{2a}\right]\notag\\
&+\frac{\rho\sigma\eta}{b}
\left[\frac{1-e^{-at}}{a}
-\frac{1-e^{-(a+b)t}}{a+b}\right],
\label{eq:cx-closed}
\end{align}
and symmetrically
\begin{align}
c_y(t)={}&
\frac{\eta^2}{b}
\left[\frac{1-e^{-bt}}{b}-\frac{1-e^{-2bt}}{2b}\right]\notag\\
&+\frac{\rho\sigma\eta}{a}
\left[\frac{1-e^{-bt}}{b}
-\frac{1-e^{-(a+b)t}}{a+b}\right].
\label{eq:cy-closed}
\end{align}
Equations~\eqref{eq:cx-closed}--\eqref{eq:cy-closed} are not a second pricing
implementation in the released code; the production quadrature integrates the
corresponding covariance functions numerically. They provide a compact
independent derivation against which that integration can be reviewed.

The deterministic part of the discount factor follows from
\[
  \int_0^{T_e}\varphi(s)\,ds
  =-\log P^M(0,T_e)+\frac12V(T_e),
\]
so
\[
  \exp\left[-\int_0^{T_e}\varphi(s)\,ds\right]
  =P^M(0,T_e)e^{-V(T_e)/2}.
\]
This is the prefactor in~\eqref{eq:gh-price}.

\begin{lemma}[Positive semidefiniteness of the joint state]
The matrix $\Sigma(t)$ in~\eqref{eq:joint-covariance} is positive semidefinite.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
For any $u\in\mathbb{R}^3$,
$u^\top\Sigma(t)u$ is the variance of the scalar linear combination
$u_1I_t+u_2x_t+u_3y_t$, hence is non-negative.
\end{proof}

\section{Executable algorithms}
\label{app:algorithms}

\subsection{European quadrature}

\noindent\textbf{Input:} curve, G2++ parameters, European swaption, order 8.
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Validate $T_e>0$, positive notional, and a schedule beginning no earlier
  than expiry.
  \item Compute $V(T_e)$, factor covariance, $c_x(T_e)$, and $c_y(T_e)$.
  \item Form $\Sigma(T_e)$ and its lower Cholesky factor $L$.
  \item For every $(i,j,k)\in\{1,\ldots,8\}^3$, set
  $Z=L\sqrt2(u_i,u_j,u_k)^\top$.
  \item Evaluate all model bonds at expiry, the signed underlying swap value,
  the positive option payoff, and $e^{-Z_1}$.
  \item Accumulate the tensor weights and apply the deterministic prefactor in
  equation~\eqref{eq:gh-price}.
\end{enumerate}
\noindent\textbf{Output:} deterministic present value.

\subsection{Coupled temporal convergence}

\noindent\textbf{Input:} common path count, seed, fine level 384, divisors
$12,24,48,96,192,384$.
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Simulate each antithetic factor pair on the fine grid with exact OU
  transitions.
  \item At fine indices matching a coarse stride, update that level's
  trapezoidal short-rate integral.
  \item Use the common terminal factor state to evaluate one payoff for every
  level.
  \item Record every level price and the pathwise difference from level 384.
  \item Update price moments and paired-difference moments online.
\end{enumerate}
\noindent\textbf{Output:} price, price standard error, paired bias, and paired
standard error for each level.

\subsection{Multi-start calibration}

\noindent\textbf{Input:} quote grid, parameter bounds, start count, seed.
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Add the documented default parameter vector.
  \item Generate remaining positive parameters log-uniformly and correlation
  uniformly within the bounds.
  \item From each start, run the same bounded coordinate search on
  equation~\eqref{eq:calibration-objective}.
  \item Retain initial parameters, final parameters, RMSE, iteration count,
  convergence flag, and per-quote residuals.
  \item Select the lowest observed RMSE without discarding other runs.
\end{enumerate}
\noindent\textbf{Output:} complete run set and selected observed solution.

\subsection{Out-of-sample LSM}

\noindent\textbf{Input:} Bermudan claim, training and valuation configurations.
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Simulate training states and cumulative discounts at all exercise dates.
  \item Fit continuation coefficients backward using
  equation~\eqref{eq:lsm-normal}; store a fitted/not-fitted flag for each date.
  \item Freeze every coefficient.
  \item Simulate a second path set with a distinct valuation seed.
  \item Apply the policy chronologically. Exercise at the first date where
  intrinsic value exceeds fitted continuation; at the final date, exercise only
  when intrinsic value is positive.
  \item Record discounted payoff, exercise date, or explicit non-exercise.
\end{enumerate}
\noindent\textbf{Output:} training price, independent fixed-policy price,
valuation standard error, exercise probabilities, and non-exercise probability.
